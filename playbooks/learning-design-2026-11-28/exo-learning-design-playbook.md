# The EXO Learning Design Playbook

**EXO Enablement's standard for every workshop and e-learning module we design or deliver.**

Built on the learning-design framework in Julie Dirksen's *Design for How People Learn* (2nd ed, 2015) — gap diagnosis, proficiency levels, pace layering, practice and feedback design — applied in EXO's own words to SDR coaching and onboarding. Dirksen's is the source framework; everything here is EXO's application of it.

Hard on the problem. Invested in the person. That applies to design too: hard on weak design, invested in the rep who's got to live with it.

---

## The one rule above all others

**Diagnose before you design. Design before you deliver.**

Most training fails before it starts, because it was built for the wrong problem. A client saying "my SDRs need training" is a symptom report, not a diagnosis. Our job — the thing clients are actually paying for — is to find the real gap, say plainly when it isn't a training gap at all, and design only for what's actually broken.

## Part 1 — The EXO Design Sequence

Every engagement, workshop and module runs through these seven steps, in order. No skipping step 1.

### Step 1: Diagnose the gap

Performance problem in hand, work through the six gap types (Dirksen) in this order. Most problems are a stack of two or three — find all of them, and be honest about which one is *primary*.

#### 1. Knowledge gap — *they don't know it*

They lack the information, or can't get it at the moment they need it.

Diagnostic questions:
- Can they explain it back to you, unprompted, right now? (Ask. Don't assume.)
- If you gave them the answer on a card, would performance fix itself? If yes, it's knowledge — and it's cheap to fix.
- Is the information available at the point of work, or buried in a drive somewhere?

*SDR examples: doesn't know the ICP, the product's three core problems, the stages of the call framework, what counts as a trigger event.*

#### 2. Skills gap — *they know it, they can't do it yet*

The test (Dirksen's): **"Is it reasonable to think someone could be proficient at this without practice?"** If no, it's a skill, and no amount of content will fix it. Only practice and feedback will.

Diagnostic questions:
- Do they pass the quiz and fail the live call?
- Have they ever actually practised this, with feedback — or only heard it described?
- What does their performance look like on rep 1 vs rep 20? (If they've never had 20 reps, you have your answer.)

*SDR examples: can recite the discovery questions, rambles or interrogates on a real call; knows the objection framework, freezes when a prospect actually pushes back.*

#### 3. Motivation gap — *they know how, and don't*

Listen for **"I know, but…"** That's the signature. Then find which flavour:

- **Doesn't buy the outcome** — doesn't believe the behaviour works or matters.
- **Anxiety** — the behaviour feels risky or exposing (calling senior people, challenging a prospect).
- **Delayed/invisible consequences** — the payoff is weeks away, the discomfort is now, and nothing visibly happens either way.
- **Unlearning** — the new way competes with a way that's worked for them before.

Diagnostic questions:
- "You know how to do this — walk me through what happens when you try." (The answer tells you the flavour.)
- What happens — visibly, this week — when they do the behaviour? And when they don't?
- Does the behaviour cost them something they care about (activity numbers, comfort, status)?

*Worked example — the SDR who won't multi-thread:*
- Knowledge? Ask them to name the three other roles they'd contact in an account. If they can't — knowledge gap, ten minutes to fix.
- Skill? Have they ever written a second-thread outreach? Role-play it. If it falls apart — skill gap.
- Motivation? They know and can, but the champion is friendly and the VP feels risky, and nobody ever sees the deals that died single-threaded — motivation gap (anxiety + invisible consequences).
- Habit? They intend to, but their morning routine is built around working one contact per account — habit gap.
- Environment? The sequencer and CRM are set up contact-by-contact; multi-threading means fighting the tools — environment gap.
- Communication? Their manager says "multi-thread" but reviews only first-meeting volume — communication gap. The rep is doing exactly what the system actually asks for.

Six different problems, six different fixes, one identical symptom. This is why we diagnose.

#### 4. Habit gap — *they can, they intend to, it doesn't happen automatically*

Knowledge, skill and motivation are present, but the behaviour isn't wired to a trigger — or an old habit fires first under pressure.

Diagnostic questions:
- Does this need to happen consistently and automatically, every time, without thinking?
- Break the behaviour down — are there habits hiding inside it?
- What's the trigger in the real workflow? Is it predictable or random?
- Is there an old habit that needs unlearning first?

*SDR examples: logging the call before the next dial; researching the account before the sequence starts; the pre-call 60-second plan; Friday pipeline hygiene.*

#### 5. Environment gap — *the workplace blocks it*

Diagnostic questions:
- If they tried to do it exactly as trained, what would get in their way?
- Do the tools, process and targets support the behaviour — or punish it?
- Is help available at the point of work (job aids, talk tracks, examples), or only in a course they took weeks ago?
- Is the change reinforced over time by anyone, anywhere?

*SDR examples: CRM logging takes 14 clicks; activity targets punish longer discovery calls; the talk track lives in a PDF nobody opens; the manager never mentions the new behaviour again after launch week.*

#### 6. Communication gap — *not a learning problem*

Diagnostic questions:
- Ask three people "what does good look like here?" Do you get one answer or three?
- Does what's said match what's measured, rewarded and reviewed?
- Has the goal actually been communicated — or just announced once, in a meeting, in June?

If this is the primary gap: **say so, in writing, and don't sell training to fix it.** Document it, hand it to the client as a leadership fix, and design only for whatever genuine learning gap remains underneath. This honesty is the brand.

### Step 2: Know the learners

Before designing anything, establish:

- **Starting point.** Novice, experienced, or mixed? (Mixed rooms are the norm — design for it, see scaffolding below.)
- **What they already know** — including the misconceptions they'll bring. Surface prior knowledge at the start; attach new material to it.
- **Their context.** Targets, manager, tools, day structure, pressures. A design that ignores the rep's actual Tuesday will die on Tuesday.
- **The stupid test.** Where in this design could a learner feel exposed or shamed? Remove it. Challenge people; never humiliate them. Nobody learns while protecting themselves.
- **Novices get scaffolding:** structure, worked examples, supports that fade as competence grows. **Experienced reps get autonomy:** resources, peer work, problems worth solving. Never run both groups through the same incline at the same steepness.

### Step 3: Set the destination — honestly

For every workshop or module, state:

1. **Objectives that pass two tests** (Dirksen's): *Would the learner actually do this in the real world?* and *Can we tell when they've done it?* "Understand the importance of discovery" fails both. "Open a call, establish the agenda, and ask the three diagnostic questions before pitching" passes.
2. **Sophistication target** (Bloom's revised taxonomy): remember / understand / apply / analyse / evaluate / create. Match it to the job, not the slide count. Most SDR work needs **apply** and **analyse**, not remember.
3. **Proficiency target** (Gery's scale): Familiarisation → Comprehension → Conscious Effort → Conscious Action → Proficiency → Unconscious Competence. **State it in the plan and in the client conversation.** A Week One workshop gets a novice to Conscious Action on call structure — doing it right, with effort, with support. Proficiency is built over Month Three by reps and coaching. We say this out loud, every time. It's the difference between EXO and every provider who promises transformation by Friday.
4. **Pace layering.** Split the content: what's **fast** (explicit rules — tools, facts, frameworks; teach directly, expect quick wins) and what's **slow** (tacit judgement — curiosity, listening, resilience; design conditions for growth over time: examples, reps, coaching). Never promise a slow skill on a fast timeline.

### Step 4: Design the experience

Build using the rules in Parts 2 and 3 below. Default skeleton for any knowledge component — **CCAF**: set the **Context** (the real situation where this gets used), pose a **Challenge** (a decision or problem, not a lecture), run the **Activity** (the learner does something), give **Feedback** as consequences (they see what their choice causes — a prospect warming, cooling, hanging up — not a pop-up saying "correct").

### Step 5: Design the environment supports

The session is not the intervention; the session plus the workplace is. For every design, specify what will exist **in the client's world** afterwards: job aids at the point of work, prompts/triggers placed in the workflow, process obstacles to remove, and what the manager will reinforce and when. See Part 2, rule set E.

### Step 6: Design the follow-up

Follow-up is part of the design, scheduled before delivery, not improvised after: when check-ins happen, what gets observed, against what criteria (the call rubric), and who gives feedback. No follow-up designed = no behaviour change promised. Put that in proposals.

### Step 7: Evaluate against the objectives

- Assess **performance of the real task** (a live or recorded call against the rubric), not recognition quizzes.
- Pilot everything on a few real learners before full rollout; watch, fix, then scale.
- Smile sheets are not evidence. Short feedback forms with free text are fine for improving the session; behaviour change is measured on the floor.
- Use success-case thinking: find who's flying and who's drowning after the training and work out *why* — that tells you whether the gap diagnosis was right.

## Part 2 — Design rules: live workshops

### A. Attention

1. You are talking to the elephant (the emotional brain) as well as the rider. Open with a story, a real call, a problem that hurts — never with an agenda slide.
2. The attention hook must be *about the material*. A funny video that has nothing to do with discovery buys you laughter and costs you the learning.
3. Attention resets roughly every time the mode changes: input → pair discussion → practice → playback. Design in segments; no segment of pure talking longer than ~15 minutes.
4. Kill monotony. Same voice, same slide rhythm, same exercise shape = habituation = phones out.

### B. Memory load

5. **Less content than you want to teach.** The right amount is less than you think. Ruthlessly cut to what this group needs for this proficiency target; everything else becomes a resource for later.
6. Chunk new material into small units, each followed by use. Nobody carries a 40-minute firehose.
7. **Shelves before stuff.** Give novices the organising structure first (the call map, the framework on one page) so incoming detail has somewhere to land.
8. Get them to *do something* with each chunk immediately — retrieve it, apply it, explain it to a partner. Information they only heard is information they'll drop.
9. Use examples **and counter-examples** (a good call opening and a bad one, side by side) — boundaries teach faster than definitions.
10. Practise under conditions that resemble the real moment: time pressure, a prospect who pushes back, noise. Calm-room knowledge evaporates in live-call conditions.

### C. Practice

11. **Practice ratio: at least 50% of any skills session is learners doing the thing.** If it's mostly you talking, it's a briefing, not a workshop. Briefings have their place — don't sell them as training.
12. Alternate input and practice in short cycles (input → try it small → feedback → go again), like levels in a game, not a straight uphill climb. Build in rests and wins; if you don't give learners recovery points, they'll take them anyway.
13. Keep practice in the flow channel: hard enough to matter, supported enough to succeed. Scaffold early reps (scripts, sentence starters, paired practice), then fade the supports.
14. Practise the *actual task* — real calls, real accounts, real objections from their market — not generic role-play theatre.
15. Vary the scenarios. Correct performance on calls is highly variable, so reps need range: different personas, different objections, different outcomes.

### D. Feedback

16. **Frequent and varied.** Feedback after every rep, not at the end of the day. Use multiple channels: coach, peers against the rubric, the recording itself (let them hear it), and consequence playback ("here's where the prospect checked out — what happened just before?").
17. Feedback is against **explicit criteria** — the EXO call scorecard/rubric — never vibes. Same criteria in the workshop, in coaching, and in the manager's reviews, so the rep hears one standard everywhere.
18. Practising wrong is worse than not practising. Correct errors in the session, kindly and immediately — unlearning an ingrained bad rep later costs ten times more.
19. Protect the person while being hard on the practice. Feedback on the work, in the open; never feedback that shames the rep in front of the room. Private corrections for anything personal.

### E. Environment and habit (in-session duties)

20. Every workshop ends with **implementation intentions**: each rep writes their own if-then plans for the behaviours ("If a prospect says 'send me information', then I will ask…"). Their words, their triggers.
21. Shrink new habits to the smallest productive version, and chain them to an existing routine (the log-before-you-dial rule chains to the dial itself).
22. Agree with the manager, before the session, what they will reinforce in the following fortnight — and what obstacle they're removing (a report, a target quirk, a CRM step). If the manager won't reinforce, tell the client the honest expected outcome.

## Part 3 — Design rules: self-paced e-learning modules

E-learning is for the **fast layer and knowledge gaps**: facts, frameworks, tool fluency, preparation and reinforcement. It does not teach slow skills on its own, and we don't claim it does. Its job in the EXO system: prepare reps before live sessions, and space reinforcement after them.

1. **Chunk hard.** One module = one objective, 5–10 minutes. If it needs 40 minutes, it's four modules and a practice task.
2. **Retrieval practice, not re-watching.** Every module ends with the learner pulling the material back out: recall questions, "what would you say here?" scenario responses, teach-back prompts. Recognition quizzes (pick A–D) are the floor, not the standard — use scenario-based questions that require applying, not spotting.
3. **Spacing is the feature.** Release and re-surface material over days and weeks (portal prompts, drip questions, Week 3 and Week 6 refreshers). A module completed once, in one sitting, is a module mostly forgotten.
4. **Scenario design:** present the situation first, ask for a decision, *then* teach. Challenge before content wherever the learner has any prior knowledge to draw on. Branching consequences beat "click next to continue" — the learner should see what their choice does to the prospect.
5. **Scaffold the incline.** Early scenarios: simplified, supported, obvious-ish. Later ones: messy, ambiguous, real. Fade hints and worked examples as the module sequence progresses.
6. **Show, don't tell.** Annotated call recordings, real email threads (anonymised), worked examples with the thinking made visible. A screen of text about discovery loses to 90 seconds of a real discovery call, paused at the moment that matters.
7. **Design for the point of use.** Modules should be findable and usable *in the workflow*, not only in a course catalogue — the 4-minute "objection refresh" a rep can run before a call block beats the 40-minute course they completed in March.
8. **Connect every module to a live behaviour.** Each one ends with a real-world task ("use this opener on your next five calls; note what happens") and, where the portal is in play, a between-session prompt and a self-rating against the same rubric used in coaching. E-learning that doesn't touch the floor is entertainment.
9. **Never make anyone feel stupid.** No trick questions, no shaming scores, no public completion leaderboards. Progress shown to the learner is *their own* growth — levels earned in gold, the rest white; never rep-vs-rep ranking.
10. **Pilot with two or three real reps** before rollout. Watch them take it. Where they hesitate, misread or skim is where the design is wrong — not where the learner is.

## Part 6 — Pre-flight checklist

No session or module ships until every line passes. Tick them honestly — this list exists to catch *us*, not the client.

**Diagnosis**
- [ ] The gap has been diagnosed using all six types, and the primary gap is named in writing.
- [ ] Anything training can't fix (environment, communication, incentives) has been said to the client plainly, with an owner.
- [ ] The problem is evidenced (calls, data, observation) — not just asserted by whoever booked us.

**Destination**
- [ ] Every objective passes both tests: real-world behaviour, observable when done.
- [ ] Sophistication and proficiency targets are stated — and the proficiency claim is honest for the time available.
- [ ] Fast and slow content are separated, and no slow skill is promised on a fast timeline.

**Learners**
- [ ] We know who's in the room / taking the module: level, prior knowledge, context, pressures.
- [ ] Novices have scaffolding; experienced reps have autonomy; mixed groups are designed for, not endured.
- [ ] The stupid test has been run: no point in the design shames or exposes a learner.

**Design**
- [ ] Practice is ≥50% of any skills session; every knowledge chunk is followed by use.
- [ ] Feedback is planned: frequent, varied, against the rubric — not end-of-day and not vibes.
- [ ] Examples *and* counter-examples are in. Real material (calls, accounts, objections) is used.
- [ ] Content has been cut to what this audience needs for this target. The rest is a resource, not a segment.
- [ ] Attention hooks are intrinsic to the material. No segment of pure talking exceeds ~15 minutes.

**After the session (designed before it)**
- [ ] Job aids exist and have a named home at the point of work.
- [ ] Implementation intentions / habit plans are built into the session close.
- [ ] Follow-up is scheduled: dates, observation criteria, who feeds back, what the manager reinforces.
- [ ] Evaluation is defined in floor behaviour, not attendance or happiness scores.

**E-learning only**
- [ ] One objective, 5–10 minutes, retrieval built in, scenario before content where possible.
- [ ] Spacing/re-surfacing is scheduled. A live connection (task, prompt, coaching link) exists.
- [ ] Piloted on real reps and fixed where they stumbled.
