# Dirksen Digest — *Design for How People Learn* (Julie Dirksen, 2nd ed, 2015)

**INTERNAL REFERENCE ONLY — not for clients, not for publication.**

What this is: the working model from Dirksen's book, in plain terms, with what each piece means for EXO's SDR coaching and onboarding work. Frameworks and concept names are Dirksen's (and the researchers she cites); the SDR translations are ours. Everything in EXO's playbook is an original application — this digest is the source map behind it.

---

## 1. The core move: learning is a journey across a gap

Dirksen frames every learning problem as a journey: the learner is at a **starting point**, the work needs them at a **destination**, and something is in the way. That something is the **gap**. The designer's first job is not to build content — it's to work out *which* gap is actually blocking performance. Build for the wrong gap and you get polished training that changes nothing: the suspension bridge built to fix a pothole.

**For EXO:** This is the single biggest upgrade to how we sell and deliver. Most clients ask for "training" when the problem is often not trainable. Diagnosing the gap first is EXO's differentiator — and it stops us delivering workshops that were never going to work.

## 2. The six gap types

| Gap | What it means | Tell-tale sign | SDR translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge** | They don't have the information, or can't access it when needed | They genuinely can't answer "what is X / what comes next?" | Doesn't know the ICP, the product basics, the call stages, what a trigger event is |
| **Skills** | They know what to do but can't *do* it fluently yet | Passes a quiz, falls apart live. Test: *"Could someone be proficient at this without practice?"* If no — it's a skill | Can recite the discovery framework, freezes or rambles on a real call |
| **Motivation** | They know how, and choose not to — or anxiety, disbelief in the outcome, or delayed consequences stop them | "I know, but…" — especially where the payoff is distant and the discomfort is now | Knows they should multi-thread / call the VP, keeps emailing the friendly champion instead |
| **Habit** | Knowledge, skill and motivation are all present, but the behaviour isn't automatic — or an old habit wins under pressure | Does it in training, reverts on the floor by week three | Logs calls properly in the workshop, stops logging by Friday; opens with a pitch out of old habit |
| **Environment** | The workplace itself blocks the behaviour: tools, process, incentives, time, no job aids, no reinforcement | Trained people *can't* do it the trained way even when they try | CRM takes 14 clicks to log a call; activity targets punish the discovery calls we just taught; no talk track anywhere near the phone |
| **Communication** | Not a learning problem at all — the direction itself is unclear, contradictory, or the goal was never actually communicated | Different people give different answers to "what does good look like here?" | Manager says "quality conversations" but the dashboard only counts dials; playbook says one thing, team lead says another |

Key subtleties from the book:

- **Knowledge is the most over-diagnosed gap.** Organisations default to "they need more information" because information is cheap to ship. Dirksen's proposal example: salespeople given four slides of product features and expected to build proposals. Information without knowing how and when to use it is gear for a hike you can't walk.
- **Unlearning is a special motivation problem.** Doing a familiar thing a new way is harder than learning a new thing — expect friction and design for it.
- **Communication gaps masquerade as learning gaps.** Sometimes the honest answer is: this isn't training's problem. Document it, don't train around it.
- **You don't have to carry all the knowledge the whole way.** Cache less-critical information along the route — supply it at the point of need rather than front-loading everything on day one.

## 3. Know your learners

- **Learners are not you.** The expert's curse: once you know something, you can't remember what it was like not to know it, so you skip steps and drown novices in detail.
- **The closet model.** Long-term memory is like a closet. Experts have shelves, labels and systems — new information has somewhere to go. Novices have a heap on the floor: everything you hand them lands in a pile. Your job is to help novices **build a few shelves first** — a high-level organiser, a roadmap, a story, a worked problem — before handing them more stuff.
- **Not feeling stupid is a design requirement.** People will protect themselves from shame by disengaging. Challenge is good; humiliation kills learning. The designer's job is to make learners feel *capable* — stretch them without exposing them.
- **Novices need structure; experts need autonomy.** Scaffold the incline for beginners (supports that fade), give experienced people resources and room.
- **Context is everything.** What else is happening in their day, their pressures, their manager, their targets — learning designed in ignorance of the learner's actual context fails on contact with it.
- **Learning styles: she covers them to debunk them.** The evidence doesn't support matching teaching to "visual/auditory/kinaesthetic" styles. Variety in how you present is fine for attention and encoding; designing to style inventories is not a good use of anyone's time.
- **How to actually learn about learners:** talk to them, follow them around (shadow the work), and test your designs on real learners early and often.

**For EXO:** Week One SDRs are novices with empty closets *and* a terror of looking stupid in front of a new team. Shelves first (the call map, the ICP picture), scaffolding everywhere, no public cold-calling humiliation rituals. Experienced SDRs in a Team Sprint get the opposite treatment: autonomy, resources, peer work.

## 4. Goals: destination, sophistication, proficiency

- **Interrogate the goal.** "Why — no, really, why?" and "What bad thing happens if they don't know this?" Sometimes there is no real problem and the honest answer is no training at all.
- **Objectives must pass two tests:** *Is this something the learner would actually do in the real world?* and *Can I tell when they've done it?* If not, it's not an objective, it's a wish.
- **Two axes for every goal:**
  - **Sophistication** of understanding (Bloom's revised taxonomy: remember → understand → apply → analyse → evaluate → create). Don't design to "understand" when the job needs "apply".
  - **Proficiency** (Gloria Gery's scale): Familiarisation → Comprehension → Conscious Effort → Conscious Action → Proficiency → Unconscious Competence. You cannot get past Conscious Action without substantial practice spread over time. A single session cannot produce unconscious competence in anything, and claiming it can is lying.
- **Pace layering (after Stewart Brand).** Some parts of any skill change fast (tools, product facts, talk tracks), some change slowly (judgement, curiosity, resilience, craft). **Fast skills** have explicit rules — you can list the right answers. **Slow skills** have tacit rules — learned from many examples and feedback over time. Design honestly for both: teach the fast layer directly, and build the conditions (examples, reps, coaching over time) for the slow layer instead of pretending a workshop installs it.

**For EXO:** This is the language for honest scoping with clients. "Your reps will leave Week One at Conscious Action on call structure — with effort, using the talk track. Fluency is Month Three, and it comes from reps and coaching, not from a longer workshop." That honesty *is* the EXO brand.

## 5. Memory and attention

- **Memory is encoding + retrieval.** Getting information *in* is half the job; designing for how they'll *get it back out* — in the moment, on a call, under pressure — is the other half. Encode it the way it will be used: in-context learning beats classroom-context learning.
- **Working memory is tiny.** A constant firehose overwhelms novices. Chunk the flow; the right amount of content is *less than you think*. Once learners have used information for a purpose, they drop it — so get them to *do something* with it immediately, or it's gone.
- **Emotion is a memory lever.** Material grounded in rich context, story and feeling is retrievable through more doors than bare facts. Matching the emotional context of practice to the emotional context of the real moment (a live call is pressured; practise under mild pressure) improves recall when it counts.
- **Memory types matter:** declarative (facts), episodic (events/stories), procedural (how-to, built by doing), conditioned (emotional associations). Use several, not just the fact channel.
- **Repetition works but is the blunt-force option** — tedious, and builds few retrieval paths. Retrieval practice (pulling it back out — quizzes, recall, teach-backs) beats re-exposure.
- **Attention: talk to the elephant.** Dirksen borrows Haidt's elephant (emotional, automatic brain) and rider (conscious, verbal brain). The elephant is bigger. If the elephant isn't engaged, the rider's intentions lose. Attract attention with stories, surprise, social proof, urgency, puzzles, shiny things — but the attention hook must be *intrinsic to the material*, or it just distracts. Attention is easiest when use is immediate (just-in-time); where it isn't, scenarios simulate the usefulness.
- **Competition and extrinsic rewards are dangerous attention tools** — they reliably pull focus from the real goal and erode intrinsic motivation. Intrinsic reward needs autonomy and choice.

**For EXO:** This kills the 90-minute slide marathon stone dead. Short inputs, immediate use, story-led (real call recordings are our stories), retrieval built in. And it backs EXO's existing stance: no leaderboards, no rep-vs-rep ranking — Dirksen's caution about competition lands in the same place.

## 6. Designing for knowledge

- Start by having learners **surface what they already know** — it gives new material something to attach to, and exposes misconceptions early.
- **Friction is necessary.** Just telling is too smooth; it slides off. Learners must engage — answer, apply, explain back — for knowledge to stick. Social interaction is a good friction source.
- **Show, don't tell.** Demonstrations and worked examples beat descriptions.
- **Counter-examples prevent misconceptions.** One good example paired with one clear non-example teaches the boundary better than five good examples.
- **Guidance is a dial, not a default.** Step-by-step directions get people through a task fast but leave them helpless when reality deviates; pure concept leaves them lost. Give enough map that learners can self-correct.
- **CCAF design loop** (from Michael Allen's work, presented in the book): **Context** (where/when/emotionally this knowledge will be used) → **Challenge** (a real task or problem) → **Activity** (what the learner does) → **Feedback** (consequence-based, showing the result of their choices).
- Success feels like the learner *invented it themselves* — confident, not lectured at.

**For EXO:** Every knowledge block in a workshop or module gets built as CCAF: a real call situation, a decision to make, an action, and the consequence played back. Discovery taught as "listen to this call — what did the rep miss?" not as a slide of question types.

## 7. Designing for skills

- **Skills = practice + feedback.** Nothing else develops them. Learners will practise with you or without you — if you don't structure it, they'll practise whatever's easiest, badly.
- **Structure practice like a game / a ride with rests, not a straight uphill climb.** Alternate new input with consolidation at the edge of current ability. If you don't build in rests, learners take them anyway (that's your phones-out moment). Balance challenge and ability — that's the flow channel; too hard is frustration, too easy is boredom.
- **Space practice over time.** Massed cramming feels productive and isn't.
- **How much practice depends on:** variability of correct performance (hundreds of right ways to run a call = many scenarios needed), tolerable error rate, how automatic it must be, and how fast they must respond. If they must react instantly in the moment (objection handling), they need reps and spaced reinforcement; if they can consult a job aid mid-task, teach them when and where to find it instead.
- **Feedback: frequent and varied.** Games give feedback every few seconds, through many channels — consequences, reactions, visible state — not a pop-up saying "Good job!" Practising wrong is worse than not practising: errors ingrain and then need unlearning.
- **Follow-up coaching is part of the design, not an optional extra.** Annual-review-style feedback is useless for shaping behaviour. Design the check-in schedule, what's evaluated, and against what criteria, *as part of the learning experience*.
- **Assess the actual task.** Have them perform the real thing, judged against explicit criteria (a rubric/checklist makes feedback consistent and lets peers and learners self-assess).

**For EXO:** This chapter is basically our coaching offer described back to us. Practice ratio, feedback frequency, follow-up schedule and a call rubric are non-negotiables in every EXO design — and "we design the follow-up, not just the session" is a sales line that's also true.

## 8. Designing for motivation

- **Two motivations:** motivation *to learn* and motivation *to do*. Different problems. The tell for the second: **"I know, but…"** — usually where the reward is delayed and the cost is immediate, or where there's no visible feedback or consequence either way.
- **We learn from experience — including the wrong lessons.** Experience without feedback teaches superstition. Design so people see the results of the new behaviour.
- **Usefulness + ease drive adoption** (Technology Acceptance Model; Rogers' diffusion of innovation): show it's useful, make it easy to try, make it observable, let people trial it small.
- **Self-efficacy is built, not told** (Bandura): mastery experiences (small wins, graduated), watching peers like them succeed (modelling), and practice at the uncomfortable behaviour until the anxiety drops.
- **Visceral beats abstract.** A real story, a real recording, a real consequence lands where a statistic doesn't.
- **Change is a process, not an event.** One workshop is an event. Design the process.

**For EXO:** Motivation gaps in SDR teams are usually consequence gaps: nothing visibly happens when you do the new behaviour, and something visibly hurts (activity numbers dip) when you try. Our designs must wire in visible feedback and early small wins — and tell the client plainly when the incentive structure is the real problem.

## 9. Designing for habits

- **Anatomy of a habit:** a learned behaviour + a **trigger** in a specific context + enough repetition to become near-automatic. No one can be *told* a habit.
- **Diagnostic questions:** Does this need to happen consistently and automatically? Break the behaviour down — do habits become visible inside it? What's the specific trigger, and does it occur predictably or at random times? Is there an old habit that has to be unlearned first?
- **Implementation intentions** (Gollwitzer): pre-decided if-then plans — *"If situation Y occurs, then I will do Z"* — specific, one per trigger situation. Deciding in advance removes the willpower cost in the moment.
- **Shrink the habit** (Heath brothers; Fogg's Tiny Habits): find the smallest productive version, establish that, then chain more onto it. Chain new habits to existing reliable ones (the vitamin next to the coffee).
- **Reduce barriers.** Ask relentlessly: *can this be made easier?* Friction kills habits before motivation gets a vote.
- **Practice + feedback still apply**, and habits with invisible feedback are the hardest — make progress visible (levels, streaks, logs) so the behaviour gets a signal.

**For EXO:** Half of SDR performance is habit architecture: the morning account-research routine, logging before the next dial, the Friday pipeline review. We design triggers and if-then plans into onboarding ("When a call ends, before anything else, I log outcome + one-line note"), shrink them to tiny versions, and make them visible. This is also the honest basis for the portal's levels/prompts design — visible progress on *personal* habits, never public ranking.

## 10. Social and informal learning

- Most learning in any organisation happens **outside** formal sessions — on the floor, from peers, in the moment of need. Formal learning suits explicit, stable content; tacit, fast-changing, complex stuff needs mentors, coaches, communities of practice.
- Communities can't be forced or bought with a platform; they grow around what people already do. Find the helping behaviour that already exists and support it; get help to happen **"out loud"** (visible channels) so one person's answer teaches twenty.
- Curation and personal learning networks are real skills — for learners and for us as designers.

**For EXO:** Call-listening culture, peer review pairs, a team channel where good calls get posted and pulled apart — these aren't extras, they're where most of the learning actually happens. Our job is to design and protect them, and connect the formal sessions into them.

## 11. Designing for environment

- **Put knowledge in the world, not only in heads.** Anything hard to remember, rarely used, or high-stakes is a candidate for a job aid instead of memorisation.
- **Proximity is the law.** The closer the help is to the moment of the task, the more it gets used. A talk track inside the dialler beats a PDF in a shared drive beats a course they took in March.
- **Job aids as training wheels and guard rails** (Ferguson, cited in the book): they let a novice produce near-expert results while learning, and they protect against dangerous errors.
- **Recognition beats recall** — a checklist of options in the moment is easier than remembering from nothing.
- **Prompts and triggers in the environment** make implementation intentions physical. But prompts habituate — people stop seeing them — so refresh them and, better, have learners create their own.
- **Clear the path.** Sometimes the design task is removing the obstacle: simplify the process itself. The simplest solution can be a line painted on the street.

**For EXO:** Every EXO engagement should leave things *in the client's world*: talk tracks in the sequencer, a discovery checklist in the CRM, objection prompts at the desk. If the only artefact is a slide deck, we failed this chapter.

## 12. Designing evaluation

- **Recall/performance assessment beats recognition.** Multiple choice mostly tests recognising the right answer; the job requires producing it. Use scenario-based questions if you must use MCQs; better, have them *perform the task* and give feedback.
- **Test the design on a few real learners before full rollout** — pilot, watch, fix.
- **Surveys ("smile sheets") are weak evidence.** Keep them short, leave room for free text, never let them be the only evaluation.
- **Consistency comes from objectives + rubrics.** Evaluate against the objectives you set (see §4), with checklists/rubrics so judgements are comparable.
- **Look for the organisation's real signals** — are the behaviours happening on the floor? Observation, interviews and case studies (Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method: find the best and worst cases and work out why) tell you more than test scores.

**For EXO:** This is the measurement spine behind the diagnostic and the buyer report: observe real calls against a rubric, track behaviour change, report outcomes — not attendance and happiness scores.

---

## The model in one paragraph

Find the real gap before you build anything (and be willing to conclude it isn't a training gap). Know who the learner is — novice closets need shelves, nobody learns while feeling stupid. Set a destination with a stated sophistication and proficiency level, and be honest about how far one intervention can actually take them. Respect memory: less content, chunked, used immediately, retrieved often, practised in conditions like the real moment. Teach knowledge through challenge and consequence, skills through structured practice and frequent varied feedback, motivation through visible consequences and self-efficacy, habits through triggers and if-then plans. Put knowledge into the environment at the point of work. Design the follow-up as part of the design. Evaluate by watching people do the real thing.
