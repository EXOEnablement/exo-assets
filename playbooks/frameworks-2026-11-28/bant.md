# BANT — Research Brief & EXO Fit

**EXO Enablement — internal framework file. Researched 28 November 2026.**

**One-line verdict:** A Week One triage tool, taught need-led and used as an internal note structure — never as the script of the call, never budget-first, and never a hard gate on a complex deal.

**Evidence status:** Unvalidated practitioner heuristic — useful as a lightweight triage checklist, not supported as a predictor of deal outcomes, and actively misleading if used as a hard gate on complex, multi-stakeholder deals. No primary IBM source or empirical validation has ever been published.

---

## Part 1 — Research brief

### Origin & source

Attributed to **IBM's sales organisation**, usually dated to the **1950s**, sometimes the 1960s. The honest position: **"IBM, 1950s–60s, exact year unverified."** IBM has never published the internal document; there is no primary source later writers can cite, and anyone quoting a single precise year as fact is repeating folklore (Yesware, "Is BANT Still Effective?", 2023; Supered, "What Is BANT in Sales?", 2026).

The context is not in dispute: expensive mainframe-era systems, cycles of months or years, finite senior rep time, and a seller who held most of the product information. BANT was a **triage rationing device** — decide fast who deserves scarce rep time. It spread through IBM practice into sales training, then into CRM stage-gates and SDR/AE handover criteria. There was no BANT book.

### What it actually is

A lead/opportunity **qualification checklist** — not a discovery method, not a closing method, not a conversation model. An opportunity counted as qualified when all four were present:

1. **Budget** — Has money been allocated, or can it be allocated, for this? Originally the cheapest disqualifier and deliberately first: a budget line, a budget holder, and willingness to spend it on *this* problem. In the IBM context, budget existing before engagement was normal.
2. **Authority** — Are you talking to someone who can say yes? It assumed a single, identifiable decision-maker who could sign.
3. **Need** — Is there a real business problem your product solves, and how painful/material is it? In the acronym and the original logic it sits third, behind money and power. That ordering is revealing: BANT was built for seller efficiency, not buyer exploration. (Modern re-orderings move Need first, because without it the other three are moot.)
4. **Timeline** (also Timing/Timescale) — When will they decide/implement? A date, a compelling event, a renewal, a deadline — or is this "someday"?

Used as originally intended, BANT is binary and early: fail one gate, park/disqualify and move on.

### Evidence base — including criticisms, stated plainly

**There is no evidence base in the research sense.** No published IBM study, no peer-reviewed validation, no outcome data showing BANT-qualified deals close at a higher rate. Its authority is longevity and adoption, not testing. BANT persists *despite* never having been validated, not because it was.

1. **It breaks in complex, multi-stakeholder deals.** Gartner's B2B buying research puts a typical complex buying group at **6 to 10 stakeholders**. Authority is then a map — economic buyer, veto-holding users, technical evaluators, procurement, champion — not a person. A single "Authority: yes" from one call creates false confidence: the deal looks qualified on the CRM and dies in a room the rep never entered. The same applies to Need: a problem existing is not the same as a committee agreeing it outranks every other priority.
2. **Buyer-side information asymmetry has reversed.** BANT assumed the seller held the information. Adamson, Dixon & Toman ("The End of Solution Sales," *Harvard Business Review*, Jul–Aug 2012) reported CEB research on 1,400+ B2B customers who had completed on average nearly **60% of a typical purchasing decision before ever talking to a supplier**. Interrogating that buyer about a budget they may not yet have created feels — and is — seller-centric.
3. **Budget and authority are often *created during* the deal, not found at the start.** The false-negative problem: a strong champion who knows the Need and Timeline but can't yet confirm Budget or Authority gets disqualified in week two — precisely the person a complex sale needs. Conversely, a deal can pass all four and still end in **no decision**, the outcome Dixon & McKenna quantified in *The JOLT Effect* (2022) at 40–60% of lost deals. BANT asks nothing about indecision, consensus, decision process or criteria — the things that actually kill complex deals. That gap is why MEDDIC and HubSpot's GPCT were built.
4. **As a conversation, it damages the conversation.** Run in acronym order on a first call — budget, authority, need, timeline — it is an interrogation serving the seller's pipeline, not the buyer's problem. Buyers notice they are being qualified rather than helped. Modern guides keep the four *questions* but reverse the practice: need first, timeline second, budget/authority as visibility built over time.

**Why it persists anyway:** simplicity (four words a Day One rep can memorise), speed (genuinely useful for high-volume inbound triage and transactional/SMB deals with 1–3 stakeholders and short cycles), teachability, and embeddability in CRM mandatory fields and SDR→AE handover SLAs. It answers a real management need — stop reps spending months on deals that were never going to buy. Those are operational reasons, not evidence it predicts closing.

**Sources:** Yesware (2023); Supered (2026); Tomba, "BANT by IBM: Still Worth It in 2026?" (2026); Weflow, GPCT comparison (2026); Datamatics, "MEDDIC vs BANT" (2026); Ziellab (2026); Adamson, Dixon & Toman, *HBR* (2012); Dixon & McKenna, *The JOLT Effect* (2022); Gartner B2B buying-group research as cited in the above.

## Part 2 — EXO fit

### How EXO deploys it

BANT has one legitimate home in EXO: **lightweight first-conversation triage**, taught from Week One in a deliberately re-shaped form:

- **Need-led, always.** The conversation order EXO teaches is Need → Timeline → (Authority as a map to start building) → (Budget as visibility to earn). The acronym survives as a memory aid; the interrogation order does not.
- **BANT is the note structure after the call, never the script of the call.** The rep writes up what they learned against the four headings — including "not yet known" — and that note is what makes the handover honest. This is the same known-vs-assumed discipline EXO takes from MEDDICC, at Week One scale.
- **The SDR's job is signal, not closure of all four.** Enough to justify an AE's time and hand over cleanly: a plausible problem, any reason to act now, and who else is involved. An SDR who "completes BANT" on call one has usually interrogated, not discovered.

### When it fits / when it doesn't

- **Fits:** inbound triage, high-volume outbound first touches, transactional/SMB motions with 1–3 stakeholders and short cycles; as a shared shorthand between SDR and AE.
- **Doesn't fit:** as a hard disqualification gate on anything complex. EXO states the buyer-reality version plainly in coaching: an SDR leader running 5+ reps rarely holds the whole budget or signs alone — finance, RevOps and a VP Sales are in the room. An SDR trained to disqualify that leader for failing Budget/Authority on call one will disqualify EXO's clients' best prospects. By Month Three, SDRs graduate to the gaps BANT leaves — decision process, other stakeholders, what a champion needs to sell internally. At Promotion readiness, BANT alone is a red flag: it signals a rep who can triage but cannot yet qualify.

### Where it lives in the Learning Design Playbook

- **Step 3 (pace layering):** BANT is fast-layer content — four explicit headings, teachable directly, quick wins. The judgement about *when not to apply it* is slow-layer, built through call reviews over Month Three. The playbook's rule — never promise a slow skill on a fast timeline — is exactly the BANT training error most teams make.
- **Part 2, rule 9 (examples and counter-examples):** BANT is taught with a side-by-side — the same first call run as interrogation vs run need-led. The boundary teaches faster than the definition.
- **Step 1 (diagnose the gap):** a team whose pipeline is full of BANT-passed deals dying at no-decision does not have a BANT knowledge gap; it has a framework-fit problem. EXO says so rather than re-training the acronym.

**Tension flagged — and it's a real one:** BANT's seller-centric origin sits against EXO's founding philosophy. End-User Experience Orientation means the prospect's experience of being qualified matters. EXO's resolution is structural, not cosmetic: BANT is demoted from conversation script to internal shorthand, and its gatekeeping use is explicitly taught against. If a client insists on acronym-order BANT as a call script, that's a communication-gap conversation (playbook Step 1, gap 6) — said plainly, in writing.
