# GROW — Research Brief & EXO Fit

**EXO Enablement — internal framework file. Researched 28 November 2026.**

**One-line verdict:** EXO's default structure for a 1:1 coaching conversation — rep self-assesses first, coach asks before telling, one committed action out the door. A conversation structure, not a proven intervention, and never a substitute for direct teaching when the gap is knowledge or skill.

**Evidence status:** Practitioner model, widely adopted since 1992, with **no adequate direct empirical testing** of GROW-structured coaching against alternatives. Its plausibility rests on (a) general evidence that workplace coaching can improve learning and performance (Theeboom et al. 2014; Jones et al. 2016) and (b) evidence for some of its component practices in other literatures. Do not cite it as "evidence-based" without that qualification.

## Part 1 — Research brief

### Origin & source — including the honest attribution dispute

GROW was not invented in a university, and not by one person. That matters for how much weight it can carry.

**The intellectual root: Timothy Gallwey, *The Inner Game of Tennis* (1974).** Gallwey argued instruction often fails because inner interference — fear, self-doubt, self-instruction — blocks natural capability. His demonstration (recounted in Parsloe & Wray, *Coaching and Mentoring*, 2000): instead of telling players to "keep your eye on the ball," he had them say "bounce" when the ball bounced and "hit" when they struck it. Attention improved without instruction. The lesson carried into GROW: ask awareness-raising questions rather than give orders. Gallwey's formula, adopted by Whitmore as his foundation: **Performance = Potential − Interference (P = p − i)**.

**The developers: Graham Alexander, Alan Fine and Sir John Whitmore, UK, 1980s.** Multiple independent secondary sources converge on this trio (Mind Tools; EBSCO Research Starter, both as synthesised in Rework, 2026), building on Gallwey, applied first in sport then with corporate clients. One frequently repeated account — that McKinsey, an early client in the mid-1980s, asked the practitioners to define their process, prompting codification — appears in coaching-industry retellings (UKCPD, 2026; TPC Leadership, 2026), not in peer-reviewed history. Treat it as practitioner history.

**The publication that popularised it: Sir John Whitmore, *Coaching for Performance* (1992**, Nicholas Brealey; 5th edition 2017). No earlier dated publication of the model has been traced (ixCoach, 2025/26).

**The naming dispute, plainly:** Whitmore claimed in 2009 that **Max Landsberg coined the name "GROW"** in conversation with Graham Alexander, and that Whitmore was first to publish it. Landsberg published it himself in *The Tao of Coaching* (1996). Alan Fine claimed in *You Already Know How to Be Great* (Fine & Merrill, 2010) that he co-developed the model with Whitmore and Alexander. Whitmore also said the model "had been in use for some time before it was given the name GROW" (Whitmore, Kauffman & David, 2013). Best-supported account: co-development by Alexander, Fine and Whitmore in 1980s UK corporate coaching, drawing on Gallwey (1974), acronym possibly coined by Landsberg, first publication by Whitmore (1992). Any source crediting one person alone is simplifying a genuinely shared, partly contested origin — and Performance Consultants International, Whitmore's firm, is an interested source for the Whitmore-centred version.

### What it actually is

A four-stage structure for a single coaching conversation — not a theory of learning, not therapy.

| Stage | Question it answers | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **G — Goal** | What do you want? | The endpoint the coachee wants, clear enough that they'll know when they've achieved it. Whitmore deliberately puts Goal *before* Reality: a goal set after examining the present tends to be capped by past performance. |
| **R — Reality** | What is happening now? | Current situation, baseline, obstacles, distance from goal — established through the **coachee's own assessment first**, before the coach offers a view. |
| **O — Options** | What could you do? | Generate possible ways forward before judging any. In some published versions O does double duty as **Obstacles + Options** (Griffiths & Kaday, 2004; Bennett & Bush, 2013). |
| **W — Will / Way Forward** | What will you do? | Convert the chosen option into committed action: "What is to be done & When & by Whom & the Will to do it" (Whitmore, 2009). Variants render W as Way Forward, What Next, or Wrap-up. |

**How Whitmore frames it — the part the acronym omits:** GROW is the skeleton; Whitmore's actual argument is **awareness and responsibility**. Coaching is "unlocking a person's potential to maximise their own performance. It is helping them to learn rather than teaching them." Good questions raise *awareness* — the coachee sees their own situation more accurately — and awareness, when the action is chosen rather than ordered, creates *responsibility*. Responsibility cannot be commanded, only chosen. That is why the coach asks rather than tells, and why the coachee speaks and self-assesses first. Used mechanically as a four-box checklist, GROW betrays its own source: Whitmore cautioned against bureaucratic use, and in skilled hands the stages are not strictly one-pass.

### Evidence base — including criticisms, stated plainly

**There is almost no rigorous research on GROW itself.** Anthony M. Grant, "Is it time to REGROW the GROW model?", *The Coaching Psychologist* 7(2), 2011: although session-structure models are widely taught, "there has been little or no debate in the literature about the use of session structures... and there have been few links drawn between the theoretical underpinnings of coaching and session structures." Grant proposed RE-GROW (adding Review and Evaluate, linked to self-regulation theory) precisely because the original was underspecified — not because it had been tested and found wanting.

The only study of GROW itself located by ixCoach's evidence review (2025/26) was Albano & Quinito (2025): eight mentee teachers and eight mentors in one school, before-and-after, **no control group described**. That design cannot show GROW caused the reported improvement.

**What coaching research does and doesn't say:** workplace coaching in general has moderate supportive evidence — Theeboom, Beersma & van Vianen (2014), meta-analysis of coaching in organisations; Jones, Woods & Guillaume (2016), meta-analysis of workplace coaching (17 studies) finding positive effects on learning and performance for coaching by internal or external coaches, **excluding managers coaching their own staff**. Neither meta-analysis tests GROW-structured sessions against any other structure, or against unstructured coaching. They license "coaching can help." They do **not** license "GROW causes better outcomes." The strong figures sometimes quoted from *Coaching for Performance* itself (an average 800% return on the authors' firm's programmes; a 74% fall in safety incidents at one client) come from Performance Consultants International — the vendor — not independent evaluation.

Components of GROW borrow strength from adjacent, better-tested research (specific goal-setting, implementation intentions, deferred evaluation when generating options) — but, as ixCoach (2026) puts it: "Goal-setting theory evidence applies to goals in general; GROW as a specific coaching model has not been isolated in randomized trials against alternatives."

**Scope criticism:** Passmore & Cantore ("Helping others to set goals: the GROW model," in *Top Business Psychology Models*, Kogan Page, 2012): GROW's goal-directed nature suits sports and business performance work, but fits less well careers conversations, person–role fit or life coaching, where exploration matters more than a target. Other practical limits, consistent across the critical literature: it assumes a willing, reasonably self-aware coachee; it fails if used covertly to steer someone to a predetermined answer; it is the wrong tool when directive instruction is urgently needed; and the many variant wordings of O and W mean two coaches who both say "GROW" may not be doing the same thing.

**Sources:** Gallwey (1974); Whitmore, *Coaching for Performance* (1992; 4th ed. 2009; 5th ed. 2017); Landsberg, *The Tao of Coaching* (1996); Fine & Merrill (2010); Alexander (2010); Grant (2011); Passmore & Cantore (2012); Whitmore, Kauffman & David (2013); Theeboom et al. (2014); Jones, Woods & Guillaume (2016); Albano & Quinito (2025); Institute of Coaching memorial (2017); UKCPD (2026); ixCoach evidence reviews (2025–26).

## Part 2 — EXO fit

### How EXO deploys it

GROW is the **default skeleton for EXO coaching conversations** — Nick's 1:1s with SDRs, and the structure EXO teaches SDR managers to run with their own reps:

- **Goal** — the specific outcome for this call block / this fortnight's pipeline work, owned by the rep, set before we look at the numbers (Whitmore's deliberate order: goals set after Reality get capped by it).
- **Reality** — what the call data and recordings actually show, **self-assessed by the rep first**. This is Whitmore's awareness principle, and it is also EXO's style in one move: the rep names the reality; the coach doesn't deliver a verdict. Hard on the problem, invested in the person.
- **Options** — the rep generates approaches before the coach adds any. Deferred judgement, per the model.
- **Will** — one committed, dated action to test before the next session. Not five. One, with a when.

The same structure scales to manager coaching: the manager's Goal/Reality/Options/Will is about *their* coaching of the team. EXO presents manager-as-coach honestly — Jones et al. (2016) largely *excluded* manager coaching from the evidence, so EXO frames it as a reasonable application of a sound structure, not a proven intervention.

### When it fits / when it doesn't

- **Fits:** goal-directed performance conversations — precisely the use Passmore & Cantore (2012) say it suits. SDR 1:1s, call-review follow-ups, fortnightly coaching cadence, manager development.
- **Doesn't fit:** when the rep doesn't yet have the capability to draw out. Gallwey/Whitmore questioning surfaces capability that exists; it does not install missing knowledge. If the diagnosis (playbook Step 1) says knowledge or skills gap, EXO teaches and practises directly first — then GROW structures the follow-up. Running all four boxes ceremonially while a rep needs direct instruction is the failure mode, and EXO names it in manager training.
- **Doesn't fit:** as a covert steering device (asking questions to land the rep on the coach's predetermined answer) — the literature flags it, and it breaks EXO's trust position instantly. If the coach already knows the answer must be X, the honest move is to say so directly. Direct feedback, including the hard conversation, is EXO's stated style; GROW is not a way to launder it.

### Where it lives in the Learning Design Playbook

- **Step 6 (design the follow-up):** GROW is the playbook's follow-up engine. The playbook requires follow-up to be scheduled before delivery, with observation against the call rubric; GROW gives that follow-up its conversational shape, and the W-stage commitment is what gets observed.
- **Part 2, rule 16 (feedback frequent and varied):** alignment with one managed tension. The playbook's feedback rules include *direct correction* — rule 18: "Correct errors in the session, kindly and immediately." Pure GROW orthodoxy would ask, not tell. EXO's resolution, stated openly rather than fudged: **in practice sessions, the coach corrects directly against the rubric; in coaching conversations, the coach asks first (GROW) and tells second, plainly, when the rep can't see it.** The mode is chosen by the gap diagnosis, not by framework loyalty.
- **Step 1 (diagnose the gap):** GROW's Reality stage is a diagnostic instrument in its own right — what the rep says about their current performance reveals whether you're looking at a knowledge gap, a motivation gap of the anxiety flavour, or simple unawareness. EXO coaches listen for the playbook's "I know, but…" signature inside the R stage.

**Alignment verdict:** clean fit, no contradiction with the playbook — provided the direct-feedback tension above is owned. EXO's style statement ("gives direct feedback, including the hard conversations") and Whitmore's ask-don't-tell are reconciled by sequence and setting, not by pretending they are the same thing.
