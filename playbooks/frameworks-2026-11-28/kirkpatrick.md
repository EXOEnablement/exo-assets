# Kirkpatrick — Four Levels of Training Evaluation — Research Brief & EXO Fit

**EXO Enablement — internal framework file. Researched 28 November 2026.**

**One-line verdict:** EXO's evaluation language, used in its New World form and planned backwards from results: agree the Level 4 outcome first, evidence Level 3 behaviour on real calls, and report Level 4 as contribution with the confounders named — never as smile-sheet success.

**Evidence status:** A durable, useful taxonomy and shared language for evaluation — **not a validated causal model**. Use it to ensure you measure beyond satisfaction, and to plan backwards from results. Do not use it to claim a good reaction score proves learning, behaviour change or results: meta-analytic evidence shows those links are weak to near-zero, each level must be evidenced separately, and Level 4 claims require explicit, honest handling of attribution.

## Part 1 — Research brief

### Origin & source

**Donald L. Kirkpatrick (1924–2014)**, University of Wisconsin–Madison / its Management Institute. The framework grew from his doctoral research in the early 1950s evaluating a human relations training programme for supervisors (dissertation, 1954 per Kirkpatrick Partners and most secondary accounts). He published it not as a book but as four journal articles:

> Kirkpatrick, D.L. (1959a, 1959b, 1960a, 1960b), "Techniques for Evaluating Training Programs," *Journal of the American Society of Training Directors* (ASTD), Nov 1959 – Feb 1960 — one article per level.

Kirkpatrick originally called them four **"steps,"** not "levels" — the levels language and pyramid imagery came from later industry use. The articles were collected in *Evaluating Training Programs* (ASTD, 1975); the canonical book is *Evaluating Training Programs: The Four Levels* (Berrett-Koehler, 1994; 3rd ed. with James D. Kirkpatrick, 2006). The model is now maintained by Kirkpatrick Partners (James D. Kirkpatrick and Wendy Kayser Kirkpatrick).

**Common confusion, corrected:** **Level 5 ROI is not Kirkpatrick.** It is Jack Phillips' extension (1990s onward). Kirkpatrick has four levels.

### What it actually is

Four categories of evaluation evidence about the *same* programme (current Kirkpatrick Partners definitions, abridged):

- **Level 1 — Reaction:** the degree to which participants find the training favourable, engaging and relevant to their jobs. Typically the post-session survey ("smile sheet").
- **Level 2 — Learning:** the degree to which participants acquire the intended knowledge, skills, attitude, confidence and commitment. Tests, demonstrations, scored role-plays, pre/post assessment.
- **Level 3 — Behaviour:** the degree to which participants apply what they learned back on the job. Measured weeks/months later by observation, manager/peer report, work-product review, system data. This is transfer.
- **Level 4 — Results:** the degree to which targeted outcomes occur as a result of the training **and the support and accountability package**. Organisational metrics the programme was commissioned to move.

Levels 1–2 can be measured at the end of a session. Levels 3–4 cannot — they require follow-up in the workplace over time. That timing difference explains most of what happens in practice.

**The New World Kirkpatrick Model** (James D. & Wendy Kayser Kirkpatrick, introduced c.2010; *Kirkpatrick's Four Levels of Training Evaluation*, ATD Press, 2016) keeps the four levels and changes how they're used:

1. **Plan backwards, starting at Level 4.** Agree the organisational result first, then the Level 3 behaviours that would produce it, then the Level 2 learning needed, then design the Level 1 experience. Do not design a course and then hunt for a result.
2. **Level 1 expanded:** satisfaction, engagement, and **relevance to the job** — relevance being the component with any predictive value.
3. **Level 2 expanded:** beyond knowledge/skill to attitude, confidence, and commitment to apply.
4. **Level 3 is the pivot — critical behaviours + required drivers.** Specify a small number of critical behaviours, and build **required drivers**: processes and systems that reinforce, monitor, encourage and reward those behaviours on the job (manager coaching and follow-up, job aids, accountability checks, recognition, work review). The New World claim: training alone does not produce Level 3; the driver package does much of the work.
5. **Leading indicators at Level 4.** Short-term observations and measures suggesting critical behaviours are on track to create the desired result — so you don't wait a year for a lagging metric to find out you failed.
6. **Monitor and adjust** throughout, rather than evaluating only at the end.

### Evidence base — including criticisms, stated plainly

The model is the most widely used vocabulary in training evaluation — and one of the most criticised in the academic literature. Both are true.

1. **The assumed causal chain is weakly supported.** Common usage assumes positive reaction → greater learning → behaviour change → results, each level more informative than the last. Alliger & Janak, "Kirkpatrick's Levels of Training Criteria: Thirty Years Later," *Personnel Psychology* 42(2), 1989, named the three problematic assumptions — levels in ascending order of information; levels causally linked; levels positively intercorrelated — and found little support for the second and third. The follow-up meta-analysis (Alliger, Tannenbaum, Bennett, Traver & Shotland, 1997) quantified it: overall correlation between reactions and immediate learning was about **r = .07–.08** — for practical purposes, no relationship. Affective reactions ("I enjoyed it") correlated ~r = .02 with learning. Utility reactions ("this is relevant/useful for my job") did modestly better (r ≈ .26, and were stronger correlates of transfer than immediate learning scores) — which is why the New World model foregrounds relevance. **The practical lesson is absolute: a good Level 1 score must never be used as a proxy for Levels 2, 3 or 4.** (Training itself works — Arthur et al., 2003, meta-analysis of 397 effect sizes, found medium-to-large effects, d ≈ 0.60–0.63. The point is the *chain between levels in a given programme* cannot be assumed; each level must be measured on its own terms.)
2. **It is a taxonomy, not a model.** Holton, "The Flawed Four-Level Evaluation Model," *Human Resource Development Quarterly* 7(1), 1996: the four levels are a taxonomy of outcomes, not an evaluation model, because a model must specify variables and causal relationships. Kirkpatrick omits the intervening variables that decide transfer — learner motivation and ability, training design, work environment / transfer climate.
3. **Bates' three criticisms.** Bates, "A Critical Analysis of Evaluation Practice: The Kirkpatrick Model and the Principle of Beneficence," *Evaluation and Program Planning* 27, 2004: (1) **Incomplete** — ignores individual, delivery and contextual influences; (2) **Unproven causality** — "Kirkpatrick's model assumes that the levels of criteria represent a causal chain such that positive reactions lead to greater learning, which produces greater transfer and subsequently more positive organizational results" (p.342) — research has not confirmed that chain; (3) **Assumed increasing importance** — higher levels are assumed more valuable, but the weak linkages mean Level 4 data generated this way don't automatically justify that claim. Bates' ethical point: relying on an incomplete model risks failing the evaluator's duty of beneficence — you can report success at Levels 1–2 while the client gets no benefit at 3–4. The New World additions (required drivers, backwards planning) are in effect Kirkpatrick Partners' response to (1) and (2): they import context and support into Level 3. They improve practice; they do not retrospectively validate the causal chain.
4. **In practice, Levels 1–2 dominate; 3–4 are what buyers care about.** Industry benchmarking cited in Arthur et al. (2003): 78% of organisations used reaction measures, 32% learning, ~9% behaviour, ~7% results (exact splits vary by survey; the pattern doesn't). ATD, *Effective Evaluation* (2019, n=779): ~83% measured Level 1, roughly eight in ten Level 2, 54% Level 3, 38% Level 4, 16% calculated ROI; isolating the effect of training was the top-named barrier (41%). The reasons are structural: Levels 3–4 need baselines agreed in advance, manager time, business data access, and follow-up months later.
5. **Attribution at Level 4 is the hard, unsolved problem.** Revenue, pipeline, retention and quality move for many reasons at once — market, territory, product, pricing, management change, seasonality, who was selected for training. Kirkpatrick provides no method for isolating training's contribution (no control group or counterfactual is built in; Phillips' Level 5 adds isolation techniques precisely to fill this gap). A Level 4 movement *after* training is not evidence it happened *because of* training. Honest reporting says "contribution, consistent with…" rather than "caused," unless a comparison design was built in from the start.
6. **Age and scope.** Written in 1959–60 for instructor-led programmes run by training directors. It fits a discrete course better than continuous coaching or an enablement system — another reason the New World version had to add drivers and leading indicators around it.

**Sources:** Kirkpatrick (1959a–1960b; 1994); Kirkpatrick Partners, *The New World Kirkpatrick Model* (©2010–2021); Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick (2016); Alliger & Janak (1989); Alliger et al. (1997); Holton (1996); Bates (2004); Arthur et al. (2003); ATD, *Effective Evaluation* (2019).

## Part 2 — EXO fit

### How EXO deploys it — New World form, planned backwards, before delivery

Kirkpatrick is **EXO's evaluation standard for engagements**, deployed exactly as the New World model prescribes:

1. **Before delivery, agree Level 4 with the buyer.** The result the engagement is commissioned to move — e.g. qualified pipeline created, first-call-to-meeting conversion, ramp time to first quota-carrying month — with a baseline and **leading indicators** that should move within 30–60 days (activity quality, early-stage conversion, call-rubric scores trending).
2. **Name the Level 3 critical behaviours** that would produce it: SDRs using the agreed discovery structure on live calls; managers running the coaching cadence; handovers written to the SPICED/known-vs-assumed standard.
3. **Build the required drivers explicitly** — manager observation, coaching follow-up, job aids, recognition — and report *missing drivers as a finding*, not an excuse. If the client won't run the drivers, EXO says in advance what that does to the expected outcome (the playbook's Step 5/6 rule, in Kirkpatrick's vocabulary).
4. **Then design Level 2 and check Level 1** — learning assessed by scored role-plays and real-task performance; reaction measured for relevance and engagement, and used to improve the session, never to evidence impact.
5. **Measure Level 3 directly, where EXO is strongest:** scored call reviews and work-product sampling against the same scorecard used in training, at 30 and 90 days.
6. **Report Level 4 honestly:** movement against the pre-agreed baseline, confounders named in the same paragraph (territory changes, headcount, campaign mix, seasonality), contribution language throughout — unless a phased rollout or comparison team gives a genuine counterfactual, in which case EXO says so and shows it.

### When it fits / when it doesn't

- **Fits:** every EXO engagement with a buyer who is paying for outcomes — which is the positioning. The backwards-planning discipline is also a sales asset: EXO agrees the result and the evidence standard *before* delivering anything, which most enablement providers never do.
- **Doesn't fit:** as a reporting pyramid where Level 1 sits at the base implying it supports the rest. EXO never presents the levels as a chain of proof. And ROI (Phillips Level 5) is not claimed as Kirkpatrick and not offered unless an isolation design exists — EXO doesn't sell a number it can't defend.

### Where it lives in the Learning Design Playbook

- **Step 7 (evaluate against the objectives):** this is Kirkpatrick's home in the playbook, and the alignment is already strong. The playbook's rules — "assess performance of the real task, not recognition quizzes"; "smile sheets are not evidence"; success-case thinking (find who's flying and who's drowning, and why) — are Level 3 thinking and a guard against exactly the causal-chain error Alliger & Janak documented. What this file adds to the playbook: the **backwards-planning sequence** (Step 7's evidence standard gets agreed at Step 3, when the destination is set — not invented after delivery) and the **required drivers** vocabulary, which sharpens the playbook's Step 5/6 environment and follow-up design.
- **Part 6, pre-flight checklist:** two lines map directly — "Evaluation is defined in floor behaviour, not attendance or happiness scores" (Levels 3 over 1) and "Follow-up is scheduled: dates, observation criteria, who feeds back" (the driver package). EXO engagements now also pre-register the Level 4 baseline and leading indicators in the blueprint's Evaluation section before sign-off.
- **Article 3 connection:** EXO's L&D article ("Beyond the Happy Sheet," drafted 28 Nov 2026) makes the public version of this argument — r ≈ .07–.08 between reaction and learning is the statistic behind it. The internal standard and the public position are the same position. That consistency is deliberate and should be protected: EXO cannot publish the happy-sheet critique and then report smile sheets to clients.

**Tension flagged:** Kirkpatrick's tidy four-box shape invites exactly the causal reading the evidence doesn't support, and clients will sometimes ask for "a Kirkpatrick report" meaning a pyramid with green at every level. EXO's answer is the New World discipline plus Bates' beneficence point, in plain language: *we'll measure all four, we'll show you each on its own evidence, and we won't pretend a happy room proves a changed floor.* That's not a limitation of EXO's offer. It is the offer.
