# 70:20:10 — Research Brief & EXO Fit

**EXO Enablement — internal framework file. Researched 28 November 2026.**

**One-line verdict:** A planning reminder EXO uses with its health warning attached — most SDR development happens on the floor and through other people, and EXO's job is to *design* that 90, not leave it to chance. The percentages are never quoted as fact, never used as a budget split, and never used to shrink formal onboarding for new reps.

**Evidence status: ⚠ EXPLICIT CAUTION.** Useful planning heuristic; **not an empirically validated formula**. The specific percentages have no controlled empirical validation, derive from rounded, recoded counts of retrospective self-reports by 191 successful executives, and should never be presented as "research shows 70%…" or used as a budget/time split. The underlying direction — most development for experienced professionals happens in work and through other people, not in courses alone — is plausible and broadly supported.

## Part 1 — Research brief

### Origin & source — three steps, and conflating them is where the folklore starts

**Step 1 — the underlying research: Center for Creative Leadership, 1980s.** Morgan W. McCall Jr., Michael M. Lombardo and Ann M. Morrison (CCL, Greensboro, North Carolina) interviewed **191 successful executives** from six major corporations about the key events in their careers and what those events taught them. Reported first in Lindsey, Homes & McCall, *Key Events in Executives' Lives* (CCL Technical Report 32, 1987), then in the book:

> McCall, Lombardo & Morrison, *The Lessons of Experience: How Successful Executives Develop on the Job* (1988)

The study identified 616 key career events, coded broadly as challenging assignments, bosses and other people, hardships, and other events including coursework. Coursework accounted for only 38 of the 616 events in the later recounting. **Crucially: the 1988 book does not contain the ratio 70:20:10.** It does not claim 70% of learning comes from experience. It is a retrospective, qualitative study of what successful executives *remembered* as formative.

**Step 2 — the ratio itself: 1996.** The numbers were first printed eight years later, in a practitioner planner, not a research paper:

> Lombardo & Eichinger, *The Career Architect Development Planner* (Lominger, 1996): "The odds are that development will be about 70% from on-the-job experiences… about 20% from feedback and working around good and bad examples of the need; and 10% from courses and reading."

Lombardo and Eichinger later explained the arithmetic (*The Leadership Machine*, 2001): they recoded the 616 events into five categories, removed adversity and personal-life experiences (about 25% of events) because those couldn't be designed into a programme, and the remaining three came out at roughly 69%, 22% and 9% — rounded to 70/20/10, in Eichinger's account, "for memorability and adoption."

**Step 3 — popularisation: 2000s–2010s.** Charles Jennings, CLO at Reuters in the early 2000s, implemented 70:20:10 there and became its best-known populariser, co-founding the 70:20:10 Institute (Arets, Jennings & Heijnen, *702010 Towards 100% Performance*, 2015/16). Jennings has repeatedly stressed the limit: "70:20:10 is a reference model and not a recipe" (2013); the Institute's own position is that "the specific ratio, in any given situation, will vary." CCL still presents it as "The 70-20-10 Rule for Leadership Development" (ccl.org, updated May 2026), with two caveats usually dropped elsewhere: not all experiences are created equal, and well-designed formal training has an "amplifier effect" on the other 90%.

### What it actually is

- **70 — Experience:** challenging assignments, stretch work, problem-solving, practice in the flow of work.
- **20 — Exposure/Social:** learning from and with other people — bosses (the dominant source in the original data), feedback, coaching, mentoring, observing good and bad examples, peer collaboration.
- **10 — Education:** formal, structured learning — courses, programmes, e-learning, reading.

**What the original research actually found:** successful executives, looking back, named challenging assignments, other people (especially bosses) and hardships far more often than formal programmes when describing turning points in their development *as leaders*.

**What it did NOT claim:** (1) it did not measure amounts of learning, time spent learning, or learning effectiveness by channel; (2) it did not test 70:20:10 as a prescription — no group was developed to a 70:20:10 design and compared; (3) it was not about all employees, roles or skills — it was successful executives in large corporations developing leadership capability over long careers; (4) it was not a budget, time or headcount formula; (5) it did not claim experience automatically teaches — hardships were a major source in the original coding, precisely the category removed to create the tidy ratio.

### Evidence base — criticisms and weaknesses, stated plainly

1. **The ratio is a heuristic from retrospective self-report, not a validated formula.** Survivors recalling, years later, what felt formative: recall bias, survivorship bias (derailed executives weren't the focus), no control group, no measured learning outcomes, no denominator for "all learning." Will Thalheimer flagged as early as 2006 that real research rarely produces neat multiples of ten.
2. **Systematic searches found no empirical study behind the percentages.** Kajewski & Madsen, "Demystifying 70:20:10" (Deakin University / 70:20:10 Forum, 2012), traced the citations and reported a "lack of empirical data supporting 70:20:10." ATD's summary (July 2014): 70:20:10 is neither "a scientific fact nor a recipe," and the phrase never appears in the 1988 book.
3. **The most thorough academic review recommends setting the 70% rule aside.** Clardy, "70-20-10 and the Dominance of Informal Learning: A Fact in Search of Evidence," *Human Resource Development Review* 17(2), 2018: "The evidential basis for the 70% rule is weak… the 70% rule of informal learning needs to be set aside." His charges: sloppy scholarship (citations citing each other back to the same 1988 study), inconsistent definitions of "informal," narrow sampling, possible leading questions. He did **not** conclude informal learning is unimportant — he concluded it cannot be left to chance and needs to be examined, structured and managed.
4. **The direction is more defensible than the numbers.** Cerasoli et al. (2018), meta-analysis of informal learning behaviours (49 samples, 55,514 people): informal learning behaviours related to knowledge/skill and performance (rho ≈ .41–.42). That supports "experience and social learning matter a lot." It says nothing about 70, 20 or 10.
5. **Ratios vary by context — by design they must.** Novices, regulated/safety-critical work and new technical skills typically need far more than 10% formal instruction before experience is safe or productive. Senior leadership judgement built over decades sits at the other end.
6. **Implementation risk: experience does not automatically convert to capability.** Johnson, Blackman & Buick (2018), studying 145 managers in Australian public agencies that had adopted 70:20:10, found it "failing to deliver desired learning transfer results," citing "an overconfident assumption that unstructured experiential learning automatically results in capability development."
7. **The budget-cut risk is real and documented.** Because "only 10% is formal," the ratio is routinely used to justify cutting formal training budgets (Harding, 2021, *Public Money & Management*, citing DeRue & Myers on the absence of empirical evidence for the ratio despite its quotation as fact). That inverts the original point: removing the structured 10% and the managed 20% makes the unstructured 70% *less* likely to teach anything.

**Sources:** McCall, Lombardo & Morrison (1988); Lindsey, Homes & McCall (1987); Lombardo & Eichinger (1996; 2001); CCL, "The 70-20-10 Rule for Leadership Development" (updated 2026); Jennings (2013, 2015); Arets, Jennings & Heijnen (2015/16); Kajewski & Madsen (2012); ATD (2014); Clardy (2018); Johnson, Blackman & Buick (2018); Cerasoli et al. (2018).

## Part 2 — EXO fit

### How EXO deploys it — as a labelled heuristic for programme architecture

EXO uses 70:20:10 for exactly one job: **reminding buyers and designers where SDR development actually happens, so the whole system gets designed — not just the workshop.**

- **The 70 (floor experience)** — live calls, pipeline work, difficult prospects. EXO's move, truer to McCall et al. and to Clardy than the folklore version: experience is *structured*, not left to osmosis. Shadowing with a brief, call blocks with a focus skill, stretch assignments matched to specific lessons (CCL's own later position: match experiences to lessons).
- **The 20 (other people)** — manager coaching cadence, peer call listening, feedback against the scorecard, Nick's coaching. In the original data, bosses were the dominant social source; in an SDR team, the manager *is* the 20. This is why EXO coaches managers, not just reps.
- **The 10 (formal)** — onboarding workshops, playbooks, e-learning modules. CCL's "amplifier effect" is the honest sales line: the formal 10 exists to make the other 90 teach. A workshop alone will not ramp an SDR; a workshop that structures the following month of floor experience and manager coaching can.

### When it fits / when it doesn't — the EXO cautions, in writing

- **Never quoted as fact.** In EXO material it is always "a rough heuristic, not a measured split." No "research shows 70% of learning happens on the job." Ever. (This matches EXO's own published position: the L&D article drafted 28 Nov 2026 debunks the ratio as folklore. Consistency is not optional — a buyer can read both documents.)
- **Never a budget or effort split.** It does not mean formal onboarding deserves 10% of the investment.
- **Inverted for novices — deliberately.** For a Day One / Week One SDR, the formal share is and should be far higher than 10%: they have no experience bank to learn from yet, and unstructured floor time at that stage mostly teaches survival habits. The mix shifts toward experience and social learning across Month Three and towards Promotion. EXO states this openly: 70:20:10 describes a destination mix for experienced professionals (its original sample was senior executives), not a starting mix for new hires. Applying the executive ratio to new starters is the single most common misuse EXO encounters, and naming it is a credibility asset.
- **Never an excuse to cut the 10 or the 20.** Johnson et al. (2018) is the citation: organisations that adopted the ratio and assumed experience would teach by itself got no transfer. EXO sells the structure that makes experience teach — which is commercially stronger anyway.

### Where it lives in the Learning Design Playbook

- **Step 5 (design the environment supports) and Step 6 (design the follow-up):** these two steps *are* the playbook's 70 and 20 — job aids at the point of work, workflow triggers, manager reinforcement, scheduled observation and feedback. The playbook was built Dirksen-first and never cites 70:20:10; the mapping is EXO's, made here explicitly so the two documents don't drift apart. Where the playbook says "the session is not the intervention; the session plus the workplace is," 70:20:10 is the shorthand buyers already know for the same idea — used in conversation, with the caution attached.
- **Part 3 (e-learning):** the playbook confines e-learning to the fast layer and knowledge gaps. 70:20:10, read carelessly, could be used to argue the opposite ("only 10% formal, so put it all in modules"). EXO's reading is the reverse: the formal share is small in *volume* precisely because it must be high in *design quality* — the amplifier has to be well built.
- **Pre-flight checklist:** a programme design that budgets all its effort into sessions and none into floor structure and manager reinforcement fails the playbook's "after the session" section. 70:20:10 is the quick language for that check in client conversation.

**Tension flagged:** Nick named 70:20:10 as one of EXO's frameworks, and EXO's own content calls the ratio folklore. Both are true, and this file is the reconciliation: **EXO keeps the model as a labelled planning heuristic and drops the numbers' authority.** If that ever feels like having it both ways in front of a client, the honest sentence is the one Jennings himself uses — "a reference model, not a recipe" — followed by Clardy's conclusion: the informal majority is real enough to design for, and too important to leave to chance.
