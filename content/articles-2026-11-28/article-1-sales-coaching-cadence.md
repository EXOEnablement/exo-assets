# Your Coaching Cadence Is a Calendar Invite, Not a Coaching Cadence

There's a coaching session in your calendar. It recurs weekly. It has "coaching" in the title.

Open it up and watch what actually happens inside it. The first twenty minutes go on pipeline: which accounts, what stage, what's closing. Then a quick run through activity numbers. Then, if there's time — there usually isn't — a call gets mentioned. Not listened to. Mentioned.

That's not coaching. That's an inspection with better branding. And SDR teams are full of it.

I've run onboarding and coaching for sales development teams globally — 300+ live workshops in 2026 alone, across five regions — and the pattern is the same everywhere: the organisations that say "we coach every week" and the organisations whose reps actually get coached are two very different groups. The calendar is not evidence. Behaviour is.

## Why does sales coaching fail even when it's "happening"?

Start with an uncomfortable number. Salesforce's *State of Sales* (7th edition, 2026, 4,050 sales professionals) found that **46% of reps rarely get feedback on their sales conversations**, and 41% say they don't get enough roleplay or practice before customer calls. This is in a world where almost every sales organisation claims coaching is a priority.

Ambition's *State of Sales Coaching in 2026* survey found the same gap from the other side: **77% of organisations have a coaching framework, but only 38% say it's adopted consistently** across managers. And only 30% of organisations coach weekly or bi-weekly — 43% are running monthly, quarterly, ad hoc, or nobody's quite sure.

So the framework exists. The invite exists. The coaching, mostly, doesn't. Why?

Three reasons, in my experience, and the research backs all three:

**1. The 1:1 gets hijacked.** Forecast has a deadline; a rep's development doesn't. So inspection wins the hour, every week, until the "coaching" meeting is a pipeline review wearing a lanyard. The fix is boring and total: separate the meetings. Pipeline gets its own slot. The coaching 1:1 is for the rep.

**2. Managers coach deals, not skills.** Deal coaching rescues this quarter's number and quietly turns the manager into a co-seller. Skill coaching — one observable behaviour, worked on until it sticks — is what compounds. Under quarter pressure, the deal wins. Every time.

**3. Nobody trained the managers.** Mindtickle's *Sales Coaching Outlook 2025–2026* (600+ sales managers) found 91% of managers believe coaching positively affects performance — while admin, not coaching, is their most time-consuming task. Gartner put the number plainly a few years ago: sales managers spend roughly **9% of their time coaching**. Most SDR managers were promoted because they were the best rep on the team. Nobody taught them to develop other people. That's not a character flaw. It's a design flaw — the same kind I write about in onboarding.

## What does the research say actually works?

Be careful here, because this corner of the internet is full of statistics that collapse when you touch them. (A popular one — "dynamic coaching improves win rates by 32.1%, CSO Insights 2025" — traces to no such report; it's a 2019 figure re-dated by roundup sites. I don't use it.)

What's left standing is still persuasive:

- **Frequency matters, a lot.** MySalesCoach's *State of Sales Coaching 2026* research found reps coached weekly hit quota at **76%**, versus 56% coached monthly and **47% coached quarterly or less**. Ambition's 2026 survey found the organisational version of the same pattern: companies where more than 75% of reps receive weekly structured coaching were more than twice as likely to report 71%+ quota attainment as companies where fewer than 25% do (54% vs 25%).
- **Consistency matters as much as frequency.** Korn Ferry (2023) found organisations with consistent coaching and impact measurement reported 32% higher win rates and 28% higher quota attainment — and around 30% lower voluntary turnover. For an SDR team, that last number is not a footnote. Retention is the whole economics of the role.
- **Quality beats quantity.** The oldest solid finding in the field is still one of the best: CEB research published by Dixon and Adamson in *Harvard Business Review* (2011) found good coaching lifts the performance of core performers — the middle 60% of your team — by up to 19%, and does little for the very top and bottom. Coaching hours alone weren't the driver. Coaching quality was. Which is why "coach more" is bad advice and "coach better, on one thing at a time" is good advice.
- **The old benchmark still holds up.** CSO Insights' 2019 study (900+ participants) put win rates by coaching approach at: dynamic 55.2%, formal 48.9%, informal 47.8%, random 41.8%. It's correlational and it's ageing — I cite it as direction, not proof — but nobody has produced a better dataset since, and the direction has never been contradicted.

One more recent wrinkle: Mindtickle's 2026 platform data shows top managers coached **45% less** than three years earlier, offloading call review and practice feedback to AI. Whether that's AI freeing managers for the coaching that matters, or managers quietly exiting coaching altogether, is the live argument. My position: AI should score the call so the human can coach the person. The moment the score becomes the verdict, your reps stop learning and start gaming the metric.

## What does a working coaching cadence for an SDR team actually look like?

Here's the cadence I build with SDR teams. It assumes a manager with six or seven reps — the Bridge Group's 2025 benchmark is 6.4 SDRs per leader — and it is deliberately unglamorous.

**Weekly, per rep (about 45 minutes total, mostly not in one block):**

1. **One coaching 1:1 — 30 minutes, pipeline-free.** Agenda: last week's single skill focus — did it show up on calls? One recorded call, chosen by the rep, listened to together. One skill focus for next week, agreed and written down. That's the whole meeting.
2. **One piece of game tape, midweek — 10–15 minutes, async or live.** The rep sends one call (or the manager picks one). Feedback is timestamped, specific, and about the agreed skill focus — not a list of everything that could be better.
3. **A five-minute brief before call blocks.** An opener, a likely objection, a plan for the accounts that matter today. Small, frequent, in the flow of work.

**Weekly, team level:**

4. **One group call review — 45–60 minutes.** Reps bring a call that worked and a call that didn't. This is calibration and peer learning, and it stops coaching quality depending entirely on which manager a rep happens to report to.
5. **A separate pipeline review.** Separate meeting, separate purpose. The moment it moves back into the 1:1, the 1:1 dies.

**Monthly:**

6. **One development conversation.** Progression, evidence, what's next — decoupled from this month's number.

And the rules that hold it together: one skill at a time per rep; the rep self-assesses before the manager judges; scorecards score observable behaviours (opener, qualification, objection handling, the ask) — never traits like "confidence"; and every focus is written down and checked next session. If it isn't checked, it wasn't coaching. It was a chat.

## Isn't this just more meetings?

No — it's fewer, shorter, and pointed. Notice what the cadence above doesn't contain: a monthly hour-long 1:1, a forecast interrogation, or a dashboard walkthrough. The total manager load is roughly two to four hours a week of structured coaching for a team of six or seven. The Bridge Group puts the median SDR manager's span at 6.4 reps; Salesforce found 40% of reps already see their manager's lack of time as the obstacle to their development. The time isn't found. It's taken — from inspection work that produces a forecast nobody believes anyway.

Hard on the problem. Invested in the person. Coaching is where that stops being a slogan: the problem gets no mercy — a weak opener is a weak opener — and the person gets the time, the specificity and the follow-through to fix it.

## FAQ

**How often should an SDR manager coach each rep?**
Weekly. The strongest current data (MySalesCoach, 2026; Ambition, 2026) shows weekly structured coaching associated with dramatically higher quota attainment than monthly or quarterly coaching — 76% vs 47% at rep level. Short and weekly beats long and monthly, every time.

**What's the difference between a 1:1 and a pipeline review?**
A pipeline review inspects deals: stages, dates, forecast. A coaching 1:1 develops the rep: one skill, one call, one agreed focus. Run them as separate meetings. When pipeline enters the coaching 1:1, it eats it — forecast always has a deadline and development never does.

**Can AI replace sales coaching?**
No — and most reps don't want it to. Salesforce (2026) found 75% of reps say they're more likely to hit targets with a coach or mentor, even as AI adoption spreads. Use AI to review calls, surface patterns and run practice reps. Keep the judgement, the context and the conversation human.

**How do I know if coaching is working?**
Watch behaviour before revenue: is the agreed skill showing up on calls? Then watch what the behaviour is meant to move — connect-to-meeting rate, meetings held vs booked, Stage 1 conversions. If you only measure the number, you'll coach the deal instead of the skill and wonder why nothing compounds. (More on measuring this properly in the article on learning measurement, linked below.)
