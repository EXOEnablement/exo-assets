# Go-To-Market Starts at the First Conversation

Ask a leadership team where their go-to-market starts and you'll hear about ICP definitions, positioning, pricing, campaigns. Ask a buyer where it started, and they'll tell you about an email they deleted, or a call they took on a Tuesday that either respected their intelligence or didn't.

Both answers are true. Only one of them is measured.

I work with SDR teams — the people who make those first conversations. Fifty-plus professionals coached, workshops run across five regions. And I'll put the argument plainly: **your go-to-market strategy is whatever your most junior, highest-turnover, least-coached employee says to a stranger at 9:15 on a Monday morning.** Everything upstream of that — the positioning deck, the brand, the campaign — is intent. The first conversation is the strategy, delivered.

## Is the SDR model dead?

It's the question every revenue leader is asking, so let's answer it with data instead of vibes.

The distress signals are real. The Bridge Group (2025) found SDR quota attainment at **60% — the lowest in its study's history** — on median quotas that had already fallen ~40% since 2018, to 10 held meetings a month. Emergence Capital and Benchmarkit found **36% of B2B software companies cut SDR headcount** last year, the steepest cut of any sales role. Ebsta and Pavilion's 2025 GTM benchmarks (655,000 real opportunities) found **78% of B2B sellers missed quota**, with average new-business win rates down to 19%.

Outbound economics have collapsed in plain sight. Belkins' analysis of 7.5 million cold emails puts the average reply rate at **0.45% of sends**. Cognism and WHAM's call data (204,698 dials) found cold-call success — conversation to meeting booked — fell from 4.82% in 2024 to **2.3% in 2025**. Hunter's *State of Cold Email 2025* found the top reason decision-makers don't reply, cited by 71% of them, is simple: **lack of relevance**.

So is the model dead? The *volume* model is. Look at Hunter's other finding: campaigns sent to 50 or fewer recipients earned reply rates of 5.8%, against 2.1% for campaigns of a thousand-plus. Small, relevant, deliberate beats large and sprayed by nearly three to one — in the same dataset, in the same year. The conversation still works. The conveyor belt doesn't.

## Why does the first conversation matter so much?

Because of when it happens and what it can cost you.

6sense's *2025 B2B Buyer Experience Report* found that **94% of buying groups had already ranked their vendors before the first seller contact**, and **95% of eventual winners were on the Day One shortlist**. Buyers initiated 79% of first contacts themselves. Sit with that: by the time your SDR speaks to a buyer, the buyer has usually done their research, formed a ranking, and chosen to test it. The first conversation is not where you persuade them you're good. It's where they check whether you are what they hoped — or quietly cross you off.

And crossing off is not passive. Gartner's June 2025 buyer survey (632 B2B buyers) found **73% of buyers actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach**. Not ignore — *avoid*. The supplier, not just the email. The same research found 61% of buyers prefer an overall rep-free buying experience, and 69% report inconsistencies between what a supplier's website says and what its sellers say. That last one should be pinned to every GTM leader's wall: the first conversation contradicting the marketing is a common, measurable, self-inflicted wound.

Bad prospecting doesn't just fail to open a door. It locks one.

## What breaks when GTM treats SDRs as a volume machine?

There's a mechanical loop here, and once you've seen it you can't unsee it:

1. Pipeline targets are set as meetings booked.
2. Reps optimise for acceptance, not fit — any meeting is a good meeting.
3. AE calendars fill with weak meetings. (The Bridge Group's own numbers show the leak: 10 meetings held per SDR per month, only 6 converting to Stage 1.)
4. Buyers learn that this supplier's outreach is irrelevant, and start avoiding the supplier — Gartner's 73%.
5. Reply rates fall. Leadership's response: more activity. The Bridge Group's median SDR already logs **112 activities a day** to produce 4.1 quality conversations.
6. Return to step 1, with a worse reputation and a more exhausted team.

Now add AI to that loop and the loop spins faster. Since February 2024, Gmail and Yahoo require bulk senders to keep spam complaints below 0.3% — a hard, enforced threshold. A human sending bad email burns a few dozen prospects before someone intervenes. An automated system sending bad email at scale burns a market — and possibly a domain. In a finite TAM, every irrelevant send spends a named account you cannot win back with a better subject line next quarter.

This is why "GTM alignment" conversations that stay at the level of slideware miss the point. Alignment isn't sales and marketing agreeing on an MQL definition. Alignment is the positioning, the website, the campaign and the 24-year-old SDR's opener all telling the same story — because the buyer is comparing them in real time, and 69% of them have already caught suppliers telling two different ones.

## So what does a GTM-literate SDR function look like?

- **Measured on accepted pipeline, not booked activity.** Meetings held and converted, qualification accuracy, AE endorsement — the numbers that describe fit, not motion.
- **Small and relevant by design.** Targeted campaigns, real research, a reason for *this* person at *this* company, now. The reply-rate data is not subtle about this.
- **Coached like it's the front line, because it is.** The first conversation is a brand interaction performed live by your least experienced people. Staff it, train it and coach it accordingly — or accept that your brand is whatever an uncoached new starter improvises.
- **Fed by marketing, honestly.** The shortlist is formed before contact; marketing's job is to earn the ranking, and the SDR's job is to confirm it. If those two teams don't share information, the buyer experiences the seam.
- **Protected from its own targets.** The moment a quota rewards junk meetings, you have paid for junk meetings and the reputation damage that comes with them. Design the target to price in quality — conversions, not bookings.

I don't argue the SDR function is sacred. I argue it's load-bearing. The companies now cutting it hardest are, in many cases, cutting the symptom — a function they built as a volume machine and then blamed for behaving like one. Rebuild it as the front line of go-to-market, staffed by people treated as craftspeople rather than dialling capacity, and the same function becomes the thing competitors can't copy: a first conversation buyers actually remember fondly.

Hard on the problem. Invested in the person. GTM is both, whether you design it that way or not.

## FAQ

**Is the SDR role dead?**
The volume model is; the function isn't. SDR headcount is shrinking (36% of B2B software companies cut it in 2025) and attainment is at a record low, but targeted outreach still dramatically outperforms mass outreach in the same datasets (5.8% vs 2.1% reply rates, Hunter 2025). What's dying is spray-and-pray, not the first conversation.

**Why is outbound not working anymore?**
Mostly relevance and reputation. Buyers rank vendors before contact (6sense: 94% had a ranking), actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach (Gartner: 73%), and inbox providers now enforce spam-complaint thresholds. Volume tactics trained buyers to ignore the channel; the channel's economics followed.

**Who should own the first conversation with a buyer?**
Whoever can hold the company's story credibly — which means the SDR function must be trained, coached and informed like a front-line brand function, not a booking desk. In practice: marketing earns the shortlist position, SDRs confirm it in the first conversation, and both sides share what they learn.

**How should SDR success be measured in a modern GTM motion?**
On fit and conversion, not activity: meetings held (not just booked), Stage 1 conversion, qualification accuracy, pipeline accepted by AEs. Activity counts (the median SDR logs 112 a day) describe effort; they say nothing about whether the market trusts you more or less than last quarter.
