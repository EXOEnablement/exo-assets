# Beyond the Happy Sheet: Measuring Learning That Actually Matters

Every quarter, somewhere, a learning team presents a dashboard. Completion: 96%. Satisfaction: 4.7 out of 5. Learners trained: all of them.

And somewhere down the corridor, a sales leader looks at a pipeline that hasn't moved and asks the only question that matters: *so what changed?*

I've spent my career on both sides of that corridor — building onboarding and enablement, and being measured on whether any of it worked. In 2026 I delivered 300+ live workshops, and I'll tell you plainly: the applause at the end of a session is the least useful data point in the building. If your measurement stops at completion and satisfaction, you don't have a measurement problem. You have an honesty problem — and eventually, a budget problem, because the function that can't show impact is the function that gets cut first.

## Do satisfaction scores tell you anything about learning?

Almost nothing, and this isn't opinion — it's some of the most replicated research in the field.

Two meta-analyses, decades apart, landed on the same number. Alliger and colleagues (1997, *Personnel Psychology*) and Sitzmann and colleagues (2008, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 136 studies) both found that trainee reactions — the happy sheet, the smile survey, the "how was the session?" form — correlate with measured learning at about **r = .09**.

Point zero nine. In plain terms: whether people enjoyed the training tells you essentially nothing about whether they learned anything. A room can love a session and retain none of it. A room can find a session uncomfortable — being scored on a live call in front of peers is not a spa day — and be changed by it.

This is the first level of the Kirkpatrick model doing exactly what it was designed to do (measure reaction) and being *used* for something it was never capable of (predicting results). Which brings us to the model itself.

## Is the Kirkpatrick model the problem?

No. The way it's used is the problem.

ATD's *Effective Evaluation* research (2019, 779 talent development professionals) found roughly **80% of organisations evaluate at Levels 1 and 2** (reaction and learning), **54% get to Level 3** (behaviour), **38% to Level 4** (results), and **16% attempt Level 5** (ROI). The top barrier, named by 41%, is isolating training's effect on results.

And this isn't improving. CIPD's *Learning at Work 2023* (1,108 respondents) found almost half of organisations lack a systematic approach to evaluating learning at all, and fewer than one in ten L&D practitioners strongly agree they make evidence-informed decisions. Fosway's 2025 research is blunter: barely **2% of organisations say they are advanced at measuring learning impact**; 51% routinely gather usage data and satisfaction scores, but only 29% always gather business KPIs.

Notice the pattern. Levels 1 and 2 live inside L&D's control: our platform, our survey, our quiz. Levels 3 and 4 require managers, business data, and a causal argument. So the profession measures where it's comfortable and stops where it gets hard — then wonders why the board doesn't take its numbers seriously.

The Kirkpatrick model's own modern revision actually instructs you to plan *backwards*: start at Level 4 (what result are we moving?), identify the critical behaviours (Level 3), then design learning (Level 2) to produce them. Almost nobody does this. We build forwards from content and measure backwards from applause.

Two famous statistics deserve a public burial here, because they prop up half the slide decks in this industry:

- **"Only 10% of training transfers to the job."** There is no such study. The figure traces to a 1982 opinion article in which a training director offered an estimate — his words were "I would estimate" — with no data collected. Researchers Ford, Yelon and Billington counted 35+ uncritical repetitions and named it "the 10% delusion" in 2011. It is still quoted in vendor decks every week.
- **"Reps forget 70% of training within 24 hours, 90% within a week."** A misreading of Ebbinghaus, who in 1885 tested *himself* memorising nonsense syllables and measured how much *relearning time* he saved — not how much meaningful material a professional forgets. The popular percentages appear nowhere in his results. Real forgetting is real; those numbers are not evidence of it.

If your measurement strategy is built on folklore, don't be surprised when a CFO treats it as folklore.

## What should you measure instead? A working stack for revenue teams

Here's the approach I use when I'm asked to evaluate onboarding and coaching for sales teams. It's not exotic. It's Kirkpatrick done backwards, with the transfer research taken seriously.

**1. Start with one business problem, in numbers.**
Not "improve onboarding." Something like: qualified-meeting rate, time to first accepted meeting, connect-to-meeting conversion. One problem, baselined *before* anything changes. The single biggest reason programmes can't show impact is that nobody captured the starting numbers.

**2. Name the critical behaviours, and build one scorecard.**
Two or three observable behaviours that plausibly move the number — the opener, the qualification questions, the meeting ask. Build a call-quality scorecard around them. Then score *practice* calls and *live* calls on the same rubric. When the practice score and the live score move together, you're watching transfer happen. When practice rises and live doesn't, you have a transfer problem, not a training problem — and now you know where to dig.

**3. Measure the transfer conditions, not just the learner.**
Blume, Ford, Baldwin and Huang's meta-analysis of 89 studies (*Journal of Management*, 2010) found supervisor support among the strongest predictors of whether training transfers to the job. Read that again: whether your training works depends substantially on what managers do afterwards. So measure it — coaching cadence happening or not, call samples scored per rep per week — as an input, not an afterthought. If behaviour doesn't change, this tells you whether the training failed or the environment did.

**4. Compare cohorts, and say "contribution", not "attribution".**
At 90 days, compare the trained cohort against the previous cohort or baseline on the leading behaviour *and* the lagging conversion. State the confounds — territory, tenure, market — out loud, in the report. A CFO trusts "conversion up 4 points in the trained cohort, with these caveats" far more than a gleaming "353% ROI" — a figure, incidentally, that circulates in sales-training marketing with no traceable study behind it at all.

**5. Design for retention instead of testing for it.**
We know what makes learning stick: spaced practice beats massed practice (meta-analytic effect size d = 0.46), and retrieval — being tested — beats re-reading (56% vs 42% recall at one week in Roediger & Karpicke's work). So stop measuring learning with an end-of-course quiz on day two and start measuring it with delayed skill checks at 30, 60 and 90 days, on real calls. The measurement *is* the reinforcement.

## What do I stop reporting?

Completion rates, seat time, session satisfaction, content views. Report them if you must — under **reach and operations**, where they belong. They answer "did it happen?" They have never answered "did it work?", and presenting them as impact is how L&D loses the room.

Do the harder thing instead. Baseline. Behaviour. Business result, with the caveats attached. It's less comfortable than 96% completion and a 4.7. It's also the only version that survives a sceptical sales leader — I know, because I've been the person whose programmes had to survive one, and the person doing the scepticism.

Hard on the problem. Invested in the person. Measurement is where you prove you meant both.

## FAQ

**What are the Kirkpatrick levels?**
Level 1: Reaction (did they like it?). Level 2: Learning (did knowledge or skill increase?). Level 3: Behaviour (did they do anything differently at work?). Level 4: Results (did the business number move?). A fifth level, ROI, was added by Phillips. The model is a taxonomy, not a causal chain — a good Level 1 score does not predict Level 3 or 4 outcomes.

**Why are completion rates a bad measure of training effectiveness?**
Because completion measures exposure, not change. Mandatory training completes at 90–100% by design; voluntary learning completes far lower — the number reflects the mandate and the format mix, not whether anyone can do anything new. It belongs in operations reporting, not impact reporting.

**How do you measure behaviour change after sales training?**
With a scorecard, on real work. Define the 2–3 critical behaviours, score practice calls and live calls against the same rubric, have managers score a fixed sample of calls per rep each week, and track the trend by cohort. Then pair the behaviour trend with the conversion it's meant to move — e.g. discovery scores up, discovery-to-next-stage conversion up.

**Can you prove training ROI?**
You can build a credible contribution case: baseline first, behaviour change second, business movement third, confounds stated. Precise single-figure ROI claims for training are usually built on isolation techniques (estimation, trend adjustment) that rarely survive scrutiny. In my experience, honest contribution analysis wins more budget than heroic ROI percentages.
